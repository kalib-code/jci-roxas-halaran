The best public speaking tips for a first-time leader come down to three things: know your one main point, practise out loud until it feels natural, and focus on your listeners instead of on yourself. You do not need a big voice or perfect English to speak well. You need a clear message, a simple structure, and enough rehearsal that your nerves have somewhere useful to go.

If you have just been asked to give a welcome message, lead a meeting, emcee a program or talk to a barangay assembly about a project, this guide is for you. It is written for young leaders in Capiz and across the Philippines who are speaking in front of a crowd for the first time.

Why public speaking matters for new leaders

Most leadership work happens through talking. You explain a plan to volunteers, ask a sponsor for support, thank partners at the end of an event, and keep a team moving when things go wrong. People decide whether to follow you partly by how clearly you speak.

The good news is that speaking is a skill, not a talent you are born with. Every confident speaker you admire once had shaking hands. Organizations like Junior Chamber International treat public speaking as a skill you build through practice, training and real opportunities to stand up in front of people.

Before you speak: prepare with purpose

1. Find your one main point

Before writing anything, finish this sentence: "When I sit down, I want the audience to…" Maybe you want them to sign up for a clean-up drive, understand why a project matters, or feel welcome at an induction. Everything you say should support that one point. If a story or statistic does not help, cut it.

2. Know your audience

Ask the organizer who will be in the room, how many people, and how long you have. Speaking to Grade 10 students is different from speaking to business owners or local officials. Choose the language they are most comfortable with. Mixing English with Hiligaynon or Filipino is fine if it helps people follow you; just keep it consistent and natural.

3. Use a simple structure

A short talk works well with this shape:

  • Opening: greet the audience and give them a reason to listen, such as a question, a short story or a surprising fact you have checked.
  • Body: two or three points, no more. Give each one an example.
  • Closing: repeat your main point and tell people exactly what to do next.

For a two-to-three-minute message, that is usually enough. Many first-time speakers try to say too much. A shorter, clearer talk is almost always better.

4. Write notes, not a script

Reading a full script word for word makes you look down at your paper and sound flat. Instead, write key words or short phrases on index cards or one sheet of paper. Write out your first and last sentences in full so you start and end strong.

5. Practise out loud, more than once

Reading silently is not practice. Say your talk out loud at least three to five times. Time yourself. Record a practice run on your phone and listen back. Then try it in front of a friend, a family member or a fellow volunteer and ask, "What was my main point?" If they cannot answer, simplify.

Handling nerves on the day

Kulba (nervousness, in Hiligaynon) is normal. Even experienced speakers feel it. The goal is not to remove it but to manage it.

  • Arrive early. Check the microphone, the stage, and where you will stand. Fewer surprises means fewer nerves.
  • Breathe slowly. A few slow breaths, breathing out longer than you breathe in, helps calm your body before you go up.
  • Talk to a few people before you start. Greeting audience members makes the crowd feel friendlier.
  • Find friendly faces. Look at people who are nodding or smiling and speak to them, then move to another part of the room.
  • Remember the audience wants you to do well. They are not waiting for you to fail. Most people will not notice small mistakes unless you point them out.

Public speaking tips for delivery

Once you are up there, these habits make a big difference:

  1. Pause before your first word. Stand, look at the audience, smile, then begin. It signals confidence.
  2. Slow down. Nerves make us rush. Speak a little slower than feels normal.
  3. Use pauses instead of fillers. Replace "uhm," "ano" and "so" with a short silence. Pauses sound thoughtful.
  4. Make eye contact. Hold your gaze with one person for a sentence or two before moving on.
  5. Stand steady. Plant your feet, keep your hands relaxed and use simple gestures when they feel natural.
  6. Use the microphone properly. Hold it a few centimetres below your chin and keep it there as you turn your head.
  7. Tell a real story. A short, true example from your own experience is more memorable than general statements.
  8. End on time. Finishing a little early is always appreciated, especially at long programs with many speakers.

Common speaking situations for young leaders

Giving a welcome or opening message

Thank the guests and partners by name, say why the event matters in one or two sentences, and set the tone. Keep it short; the audience came for the main program.

Emceeing a program

Your job is to keep things moving and make speakers look good. Prepare a run sheet, confirm the correct names and titles of everyone you will introduce, and have short "filler" lines ready in case of delays.

Presenting a project proposal

Start with the problem, then your solution, what you need, and what success will look like. If you are writing the proposal too, our community project proposal sample and event proposal template will help you organize your points before you present them.

Running a meeting

Share the agenda at the start, keep time, invite quieter members to speak, and summarize decisions at the end. This is public speaking too, just in a smaller room.

After you speak: keep improving

Right after your talk, write down what went well and one thing to improve. Ask a trusted friend for honest feedback. Then look for your next chance to speak. The fastest way to grow is regular practice: volunteer to emcee, give a report at your next meeting, or join a group that gives you a stage.

Speaking is one of several skills that make a good leader. For the bigger picture, read our guides on the qualities of a good leader and the different types of leadership.

Frequently asked questions

How can I stop shaking when I speak in public?

Prepare well, arrive early, and take a few slow breaths before you start. Plant your feet and hold your notes or the microphone with both hands at first. Shaking usually fades after the first minute once you get going.

Should I memorize my speech?

Memorize your opening and closing lines, but not the whole speech. Use key-word notes for the middle so you can speak naturally and recover easily if you lose your place.

How long should a welcome message be?

Usually two to three minutes is enough, unless the organizer asks for more. Always ask how much time you have and stay within it.

Is it okay to speak in Hiligaynon or Filipino instead of English?

Yes. Use the language your audience understands best. Clarity matters more than the language you choose.
