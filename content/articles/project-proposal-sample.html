A project proposal is a document that explains a problem, the project you plan to run to address it, who it will help, how much it will cost and how you will measure success. The standard sections are: title page, project summary, background and rationale, objectives, target beneficiaries, activities, timeline, budget, monitoring and evaluation, sustainability, and signatories. Below is a complete project proposal sample for a community project, written as a fill-in-the-blanks template you can copy. It works for youth organizations, student councils, barangay groups and non-profits in the Philippines.

Replace everything in [square brackets] with your own details. The example notes in parentheses show what a finished answer might look like. Delete them before you submit.

Before you write: three quick questions

  • Who will read it? A barangay council, school administrator, LGU office, sponsor or grant-giver. Each cares about different things, so adjust your emphasis.
  • What problem are you solving? If you can't say it in one sentence, the proposal will be hard to write.
  • What does success look like? Pick numbers you can actually count: people trained, kilos collected, trees planted.

Project proposal sample (copy and fill in)

1. Title page

Project title: [Short, clear name, e.g. "Project [Name]: [What it does] for [Who]"]
Proponent: [Organization name]
Address: [Street, barangay, city or municipality, province]
Contact person: [Full name], [Position]
Mobile / email: [Mobile number] / [Email address]
Project location: [Barangay, city or municipality]
Project duration: [Start date] to [End date]
Total project cost: PhP [amount]
Amount requested: PhP [amount] [or in-kind items]
Date submitted: [Date]

2. Project summary

[Organization name] proposes to conduct [project title], a [one-day / one-month / six-month] project that will [main activity] for [number] [beneficiaries] in [location]. The project addresses [problem in one sentence]. It will run from [start date] to [end date] with a total cost of PhP [amount], of which PhP [amount] is requested from [recipient of the proposal].

(Example: "[Youth organization name] proposes a one-day coastal cleanup and waste-segregation seminar for 100 youth volunteers in Barangay [Name], addressing plastic waste along the shoreline.")

3. Background and rationale

[Paragraph 1: Describe the problem. Who is affected, where, and how? Use facts you can back up, such as barangay data, school records, a survey you conducted, or official statistics. Cite your source.]

[Paragraph 2: Explain why it matters now. What happens if nothing is done?]

[Paragraph 3: Explain why your organization is the right group to do this. Mention relevant past projects, skills or partners, only if true.]

[Optional: Link the project to a bigger goal, such as a barangay development plan or one of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, e.g. "This project supports SDG [number]: [goal name]."]

4. Objectives

General objective: To [overall change you want, e.g. "reduce plastic waste along the coastline of Barangay [Name]"].

Specific objectives. By the end of the project, we aim to:

  1. [Action verb + measurable result + deadline, e.g. "Train [number] youth volunteers on proper waste segregation by [date]."]
  2. [e.g. "Collect at least [number] kilograms of waste from [length] of shoreline."]
  3. [e.g. "Set up [number] labeled segregation bins with the barangay by [date]."]

Tip: good objectives are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound (SMART).

5. Target beneficiaries

  • Direct beneficiaries: [Number] [who, e.g. "public high school students aged 13 to 17"]
  • Indirect beneficiaries: [Who else benefits, e.g. "families and fisherfolk in Barangay [Name]"]
  • Selection criteria: [How you choose participants, e.g. "nominated by the school guidance office"]

6. Project description and activities

[One paragraph describing what will happen, in order.]

  1. Pre-project: [e.g. coordination with barangay officials, securing permits, recruiting volunteers, buying supplies]
  2. Activity 1: [Name, description, who leads it, how many join]
  3. Activity 2: [Name, description, who leads it, how many join]
  4. Activity 3: [Name, description, who leads it, how many join]
  5. Post-project: [e.g. evaluation, documentation, report to partners, thank-you to sponsors]

  • [Month / week 1]: [Planning, coordination, permits]

  • [Month / week 4]: [Evaluation and reporting]

  • Secretary: [Name], documentation, attendance, minutes

  • Logistics: [Name], venue, supplies, transport

11. Sources of funds

  • [Organization funds]: PhP [amount]

12. Monitoring and evaluation

  • Indicators: [What you will count, e.g. number of attendees, kilos of waste collected, pre- and post-test scores]

13. Sustainability

[How will the benefits continue after the project ends? e.g. "Trained volunteers will form a youth environment group that meets monthly," or "The barangay has agreed to maintain the bins."]

Tips to make your project proposal stand out

  • Lead with the problem, not your organization. Readers fund results, not logos.
  • Use real numbers. "100 students in Barangay [Name]" is stronger than "many youth."
  • Keep the budget honest. Inflated costs damage trust. Get quotes from at least two suppliers if you can.
  • Coordinate early. Talk to the barangay, school or LGU office before you submit. A proposal they already expect is easier to approve.
  • Connect to bigger goals. Linking your project to a local plan or the UN Sustainable Development Goals shows the reader you see the bigger picture.
  • Proofread and format. Use headings, page numbers and your letterhead. Ask someone outside your group to read it once.
  • Report back. A clear post-activity report with photos and liquidation makes your next proposal much easier to approve.

Frequently asked questions

What are the parts of a project proposal?

Most include a title page, summary, background and rationale, objectives, target beneficiaries, activities, timeline, budget, sources of funds, monitoring and evaluation, sustainability, and signatories.

How long should a project proposal be?

For a small community project, three to eight pages is common. Follow any page limit or format the receiving office gives you.

What is the difference between a project proposal and an event proposal?

A project proposal covers a full initiative that may run over weeks or months. An event proposal focuses on a single event, such as a seminar, fun run or fair, and its program and logistics.

What is a rationale in a project proposal?

It is the "why" section. It explains the problem, who is affected, why it matters now, and why your group is the right one to act.
